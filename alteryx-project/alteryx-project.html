Global Retail & Profitability Analytics

Alteryx · Tableau · Customer Segmentation · Retail Profitability

01 · Project Overview

How can a multinational retailer improve sales performance by understanding its customers, developing more relevant product offers, and addressing costly returns?

Using the Global Superstore dataset, our team explored business performance across markets. My individual contribution focused on four connected questions: who the retailer serves, where repeat purchasing is strongest, which product categories offer potential cross-selling opportunities, and how returns relate to profitability. I used Alteryx to prepare and transform transactional data and Tableau to visualize patterns and communicate recommendations.

02 · Business Challenge

Increasing sales is not only about acquiring more customers. Retailers also need to understand the mix of customers generating revenue, whether customers return to purchase again, how products could be marketed together, and whether returns undermine margins. These questions require combining transaction-level records with customer, product, regional, and return information.

My guiding business question: How can customer and transaction analytics inform targeted retention efforts, potential cross-selling strategies, and return-reduction priorities?

03 · Dataset & Analytical Assumptions

The analysis used a Global Superstore retail dataset containing more than 50,000 multinational transactions. The primary analytical source was the Orders tab, with the Returns tab used to identify returned orders. Relevant dimensions included customer, region, product category and subcategory, order identifiers, sales, and profit.

Before interpreting results, I worked from the assumption that the source data was sufficiently clean, complete, and accurate for exploratory analysis. In practice, those assumptions should be tested by checking identifier consistency, missing values, duplicate records, and whether Orders and Returns can be joined reliably.

Interpretation limits: Repeat purchasing is a transaction-based proxy for loyalty, not a direct measure of customer satisfaction. Cross-sell candidates require order-level co-purchase validation before being described as frequently bought together. Return and profit comparisons show associations, not necessarily the causal effect of a return.

04 · My Role & Analytical Approach

I was responsible for four analyses within the broader team project. My workflow followed a consistent process:

  1. Frame the problem: Define business questions tied to improving retail sales performance.
  2. Review the data: Inspect available tables, relevant fields, relationships, and assumptions about data quality.
  3. Transform in Alteryx: Select fields, summarize records, create classifications, join datasets, and export analysis-ready outputs.
  4. Visualize in Tableau: Select charts suited to each question, including treemaps, bar charts, a cross-sell heatmap, and stacked bars.
  5. Interpret and recommend: Translate observed patterns into testable customer, product, and returns strategies, noting limitations and follow-up analysis.

05 · Analysis & Findings

01 · Customer mix

Customer Segmentation

Business question: Which customer segments contribute most to sales and profit?

Approach: Compared the Consumer, Corporate, and Home Office segments using sales and profitability measures. In Tableau, I used a treemap to communicate each segment's relative contribution.

Finding: Consumer was the largest segment, contributing approximately 51.4% of sales in the presentation, while Corporate and Home Office contributed approximately 30.3% and 18.3%. This suggests Consumer is an important audience for growth initiatives, but segment size alone does not establish which customers have the highest lifetime value.

Recommendation: Prioritize Consumer-focused campaigns while comparing profit margins and repeat purchasing before reallocating marketing investment.

02 · Customer retention

One-Time vs. Repeat Customers by Region

Business question: Where does the retailer have a stronger base of returning customers, and where might retention initiatives matter most?

Alteryx workflow: Import Orders → select customer and regional fields → summarize order counts per customer and region → create a Buyer_Type classification (one-time or repeat) → summarize customer counts by region and buyer type → export for Tableau.

Visualization: A regional stacked bar chart comparing the share of one-time and repeat buyers.

Finding: The team presentation reported that one-time buyers represented roughly 70–90% of customers in many regions. Western Europe showed a comparatively stronger repeat-customer base, at approximately 56% repeat buyers, while Central Asia and Eastern Africa showed especially high one-time-buyer shares.

Recommendation: Investigate whether differences in acquisition channels, product mix, delivery experience, or customer cohorts explain regional variation before testing region-specific retention campaigns.

03 · Product opportunities

Cross-Selling Potential Across Product Subcategories

Business question: Which subcategory combinations could be candidates for product bundles or related-product promotions?

Alteryx workflow: Import Orders → select relevant product and sales fields → summarize sales by subcategory → self-join the summarized data → filter mirrored or duplicate pairs → calculate a combined pair-sales measure → summarize and export the resulting candidate pairs.

Visualization: A Tableau heatmap comparing potential subcategory pairings.

Recommendation: Use the heatmap as an initial way to prioritize possible bundles and in-cart recommendations, then validate candidate combinations using actual shared Order IDs before claiming that customers frequently purchase those products together.

Methodological note: The documented self-join combines subcategory sales totals; it is not, on its own, an order-level market-basket analysis. A stronger next iteration would self-join order lines on Order ID and calculate pair frequency, support, and lift.
04 · Profit protection

Returns & Profitability Analysis

Business question: Which product categories and subcategories warrant attention because of return patterns and weaker profitability?

Alteryx workflow: Import Orders and Returns → join on Order ID → create a returned/not-returned flag → combine returned and non-returned records → summarize profitability by category → calculate return-rate measures for Tableau.

Visualizations: A stacked bar chart comparing profitability across Furniture, Office Supplies, and Technology, alongside a bar chart of return rates by subcategory.

Findings: The presentation reported lower average profit for returned transactions in Technology and Furniture. It also highlighted Labels (5.16%) and Tables (4.55%) among the highest subcategory return rates. Office Supplies showed a small positive difference in the comparison, which should not be interpreted as proof that returns improve profitability.

Recommendation: Review high-return products and investigate potential drivers such as product expectations, quality, shipping damage, or fulfillment accuracy. Prioritize changes based on both return rate and financial exposure.

06 · Business Recommendations

  • Customer strategy: Use segment contribution and regional repeat-purchase patterns to guide targeted retention experiments.
  • Cross-selling: Validate high-potential subcategory combinations at the order level, then pilot bundles or recommendation placements.
  • Returns: Investigate high-return subcategories and focus interventions where return volume and profit exposure are both meaningful.
  • Measurement: Track repeat-purchase rate, campaign conversion, average order value, return rate, and contribution margin to evaluate whether proposed actions improve performance.

07 · Limitations & Next Steps

This was an exploratory business intelligence project rather than a live operational deployment. The analysis identifies patterns and possible actions, but it does not demonstrate that the recommendations increased sales or reduced returns.

  • Validate data quality: Reconcile order counts and return joins, inspect missing fields, and confirm denominator definitions.
  • Improve loyalty measurement: Add customer tenure, cohort analysis, and repeat-purchase timing.
  • Strengthen cross-sell analysis: Identify products appearing in the same orders and assess association-rule metrics such as support, confidence, and lift.
  • Measure return economics: Include refunds, restocking, shipping, and product costs where available.
  • Test recommendations: Run small-scale campaigns or operational pilots and compare results against a baseline.
