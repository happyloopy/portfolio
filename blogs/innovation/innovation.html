INNOVATION... What about it?

My visit to The Ford Rouge Factory and Henry Ford Museum demonstrates that successful innovation is not a single event but a continuous organizational process that aims to reduce production costs, increase customer value, and adapt to changing competitive pressures.

Innovation is Fundamentally Human-centered

Across every field, not just business, innovation is created by humans and for humans. Since Ford understands deeply that innovation cannot succeed if there is an absence of spirit, its commitment to its employees extends beyond productivity to creating an environment where people can perform at their best. This can be traced back to Ford's landmark $5 workday in 1914, which transformed industrial labor, and later to its collaboration with the United Auto Workers in the 1940s to improve working conditions and strengthen employee welfare. Today, this people-first mindset remains evident throughout the factory: from the winged glass roof to help workers adapt the environment to seasonal conditions, to the Universal EV Production System, which reimagines the assembly line to simplify work and reduce physical demands on employees. Therefore, Ford's long-standing principle that "Quality Is Job 1" reflects its belief that empowered humans are the backbone of lasting innovations.

Innovation in Business

From a business standpoint, the trip also reinforces our class discussion that innovation is a two-way street: reducing costs and improving efficiency backstage, and increasing customer value frontstage. For example, I observed various process innovations in the factory that supported this principle, such as the moving conveyor system, specialized 52-second workstations, and real-time production dashboards. As Henry Ford claimed in the Theater 1 short movie, the goal was to move "the work to the man" rather than "the man to the work," which meant minimizing unnecessary motion to boost productivity. On the other hand, innovation equals value. When I walked around the car models at Ford Factory, I realized that every successful Ford vehicle represented a different answer to the same question: "What do customers need now that they did not need before?" Indeed, the exhibits showed that Ford never stopped reinventing its vehicles as customer expectations evolved - from making automobiles affordable and improving their performance, comfort, styling (Model A, Model T, Ford V8), to creating an entirely new market segment such as the Mustang. Similarly, during the Theater 2 presentation, I noticed that in the modern time, Ford approaches innovation by first understanding what customers dislike and asking what jobs its customers want to accomplish. We agreed in class that innovations can fail when they introduce something new without solving a meaningful customer problem. Therefore, inventions can only become true innovations when they successfully fulfill a customer's "job to be done."

Innovation is Driven by Competition

Ford continuously reinvents its products and processes to stay ahead of its competitors, maintaining both comparative and competitive advantage to always achieve superior performance. Thus, in this sense, competition acts as the driving force that transforms innovation from a choice into a necessity.

Conclusion: Innovation is Never Ending!

Throughout the Ford Rouge Factory, I observed that production processes are constantly refined through trial and error and flexible manufacturing. Even the process map right at the gate stated that "flow is subject to change," consistent with the idea that innovation is dynamic. In class, we learned that Schumpeter described innovation as a process of "creative destruction," which meant that older systems would be continually replaced by new ones. As I left the Ford Rouge Factory, I also saw a quote by Henry Ford: "Thinking always ahead, thinking always of trying to do more, brings a state of mind in which nothing is impossible." Together, these observations convinced me that innovation is not only a process but also a mindset. It should not be considered "the end goal" by a company, but rather a continuous journey of learning, adapting, and reinventing in response to the ever-changing business contexts.
