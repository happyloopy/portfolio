01 · Project Overview

How can a manufacturer improve profitability without compromising production reliability, inventory availability, or customer service? In The Fresh Connection, our four-person team managed a simulated orange juice company across eight decision rounds. As VP of Operations, I focused on warehouse capacity, staffing, production equipment, and the tradeoffs behind each operational improvement.

02 · My Role: VP of Operations

I managed decisions affecting the raw-materials and finished-goods warehouses, inbound staffing, bottling capacity, production equipment, and process reliability. My work depended on coordinating with teammates responsible for purchasing, sales, and supply chain planning: a warehouse decision that reduced cost could still create problems if supplier lead times, safety stock, or demand changed.

From Experimentation to Execution

Phase 1 · Rounds 1–4: We tested changes and learned how connected the simulation's financial and operational KPIs were. I adjusted warehouse capacity based on utilization and the supply chain team's inventory plans. Increasing bottling shifts from two to three improved production plan adherence from 78.7% to 95.3%, but introduced roughly 27.9% unused capacity and an additional 100,000 in costs. We returned to two shifts and invested in changeover and speed improvements instead.

Phase 2 · Rounds 5–8: We shifted toward a coordinated strategy, evaluating supplier quality, warehouse labor, packaging configuration, outsourcing, equipment selection, and maintenance together. The team's final ROI reached 11.21%.

03 · Operations Decisions & Tradeoffs

01 · Warehouse staffing

Extended inbound intake time from five to seven days to smooth workload peaks, then reduced permanent inbound staffing from five to three, two, and finally one employee as the observed workload allowed.

02 · Packaging footprint

Adopted inflatable PET bottle preforms, which used about 90% fewer pallet locations than finished bottles. The space and handling savings came with a substantial upfront investment and recurring inflation costs.

03 · Finished-goods distribution

Outsourced the finished-goods warehouse to a multi-client logistics center to replace some fixed warehousing costs with usage-based charges and improve flexibility as demand fluctuated.

04 · Production reliability

Compared mixer and bottling-line alternatives on cost, capacity, changeover time, and tolerance. Retained the existing mixer, selected MultiFlex 1 for bottling flexibility, and introduced inspection, breakdown training, and later preventive maintenance.

04 · Selected Outcomes

  • Warehouse cost improvement in Phase 1: Raw-materials warehousing costs declined from 232,176 to 189,784, while finished-goods warehousing costs declined from 234,331 to 177,987 in the team's first debrief.
  • Production planning: The initial increase in bottling shifts demonstrated that higher adherence can carry substantial unused-capacity and labor costs, making the tradeoff—not just the KPI—central to our decisions.
  • Inbound efficiency in Phase 2: Staffing was reduced from five to one permanent employee while the decision rationale reported stable inbound operations.
  • Financial performance: The team ended Round 8 with an 11.21% ROI after coordinating decisions across operations, purchasing, sales, and supply chain.

07 · Reflection

My biggest takeaway was that operational efficiency is not simply about reducing costs. Adding a bottling shift improved production adherence, but the resulting unused capacity made the decision less attractive financially. Likewise, switching to inflatable PET bottles saved warehouse space while requiring an upfront investment. The simulation taught me to evaluate the full cost and operational impact of each choice, revisit assumptions after every round, and work across functions rather than optimizing my department in isolation.
