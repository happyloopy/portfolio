01 · The Business Challenge

The client operated across multiple electronic medical record (EMR) and revenue cycle management systems. The two systems examined in our proof of concept exported operational data in incompatible structures. Differences in column names, date formats, payer labels, identifiers, and missing values made even basic comparisons difficult.

Analysts were spending substantial time manually cleaning and reconciling extracts before they could answer questions about claim charges, payments, or monthly trends. Without shared definitions, reports from different affiliates could not always be compared reliably.

Before

  • Disconnected source files and inconsistent schemas
  • Repeated manual cleanup and reconciliation
  • Unclear or mismatched field definitions
  • Limited confidence in cross-system reporting

Target state

  • Repeatable, validated transformation workflows
  • Shared definitions for core data domains
  • Canonical patient, claim, and payment tables
  • Comparable KPIs and scalable analytics

02 · Dataset & Scope

Our team assessed and processed 400+ healthcare operational files, representing approximately five million rows across the working datasets. The proof of concept focused on two clinical systems and three high-value data domains: patients, claims, and payments. Source extracts included text files and structured CSV/Excel exports.

Patients

Identifiers and selected demographic fields, requiring normalization of names, dates, categories, and test records.

Claims

Claim identifiers, charge amounts, and dates, requiring consistent numeric and date handling.

Payments

Payment IDs, dates, amounts, methods, and payer attributes, requiring standardized classifications and payer mappings.

Data quality

Missing values, malformed records, duplicates, inconsistent delimiters, and conflicting field definitions.

Dataset sizes and file counts are approximate project-level figures.

04 · Technical Approach

We used a layered process to separate raw data preparation from business-rule standardization and final integration. This separation made the work easier to test, explain, and reuse.

Source exportsRaw cleaningSemantic cleaningCanonical mappingBI & reporting

Step 1 · Raw extraction and structural cleaning

One source system provided structured files; the other required more extensive preprocessing of pipe-delimited text exports. My Python application accepted a selected folder, created a cleaned-output subfolder, processed each eligible text file, and generated a consolidated quality report.

Key checks included blank-line removal, byte-order-mark cleanup, preservation of empty pipe-delimited fields, handling malformed quotation marks, header normalization, and parsing with Pandas. Records that could not be parsed were tracked instead of silently treated as valid.

Step 2 · Field and record validation

The workflow trimmed whitespace, normalized empty and NULL values, removed fully empty rows or columns, and recorded input rows, successfully loaded rows, skipped rows, and missing-value percentages. These metrics helped users evaluate whether an output was suitable for downstream processing.

Step 3 · Semantic standardization

Using a shared data dictionary and crosswalk, our team applied domain-specific rules: consistent date formats, numeric amount validation, payer and payment-method normalization, removal of test patient records, and duplicate-ID handling. This step aligned fields with equivalent business meanings across both systems.

Step 4 · Canonical integration

We built three standardized outputs—patients_canonical.csv, payments_canonical.csv, and claims_canonical.csv. Source-system flags preserved provenance, while shared schemas supported cross-system comparison, SQL data exploration, and Power BI reporting.

05 · A Closer Look at the Python Cleaner

My batch-cleaning tool was designed for analysts who needed repeatable preprocessing without manually editing hundreds of files. The graphical interface displayed progress, an estimated completion time, and a running log, then provided access to the output folder and quality report.

  • Read each text export line by line and skip blank lines.
  • Remove hidden BOM characters when present.
  • Preserve consecutive pipe delimiters, which indicate empty fields.
  • Handle irregular quote counts while preserving valid quoted fields.
  • Parse pipe-delimited data with Pandas and warn on malformed rows.
  • Standardize whitespace, quotation marks, and missing-value tokens.
  • Remove completely empty records and fields.
  • Track raw row counts, loaded rows, skipped rows, and missing percentages.
  • Write a cleaned CSV for each processed file and a summary quality report.
  • Surface run status and output locations through the GUI.
  • Payments: normalize dates and amounts, standardize method labels, map payer attributes, and deduplicate payment IDs.
  • Patients: normalize demographics and date formats, exclude test records, and standardize categorical values.
  • Claims: align claim IDs, amounts, and posting dates to a shared output schema.

These examples summarize transformation categories without exposing confidential client data.

07 · Analytics & Business Value

After standardization, our team used SQL to query and analyze the integrated patient, claims, and payment datasets, and developed interactive Power BI proof-of-concept dashboards to demonstrate how previously incompatible data could support comparable reporting across systems. Example KPIs included total claim charges, payments by method or system, and monthly claim trends. We also outlined opportunities for governance, data onboarding, and future forecasting.

Efficiency

The project reported an approximately 80% reduction in manual data preparation effort, helping analysts move more quickly from raw extracts to usable datasets.

Financial opportunity

The team estimated ~$1.3 million in potential annual savings through an illustrative cost-benefit model. This was a projection, not verified realized savings.

Reporting consistency

Common definitions and canonical datasets enabled more meaningful comparisons between the two source systems.

Scalability

The locally implemented proof of concept was designed with future cloud orchestration and additional affiliates in mind; enterprise deployment was a recommendation, not a completed rollout.

Savings estimate: the team's cost-benefit analysis assumed a 10% reduction against a cited industry-wide $12.9M annual poor-data-quality cost benchmark. It is not a client-specific audited financial outcome.

08 · Recommendations & Next Steps

  • Assign data owners and maintain a shared data dictionary and KPI definitions.
  • Introduce controlled review and versioning for transformation and mapping changes.
  • Schedule automated ETL runs and strengthen validation as more affiliates are onboarded.
  • Promote standardized reporting workflows and provide training for analysts.
  • Evaluate future predictive use cases, such as demand or revenue forecasting, only after the data foundation is sufficiently governed and validated.
